# Strength standards and per-body-part level ranking systems (implementation notes)

Scope: how Strength Level, Symmetric Strength, ExRx and gym apps (Hevy, Boostcamp, Fitbod, Liftoff, Stronger, Alpha Progression, Strong) compute strength standards and "levels" from bodyweight, plus the powerlifting normalisation formulas (Wilks, DOTS, IPF GL, Sinclair, allometric), age/sex handling, licensing, and presentation. Target lifter for worked examples: male, 179 cm, 73 kg, metric. Research date: 2026-09-26. All numbers below are quoted from the cited pages/code as fetched on that date; my own arithmetic is labelled "computed".

---

## Q1. How do Strength Level, Symmetric Strength and ExRx define their levels, and what are the thresholds for a ~73 kg male?

### Takeaway
Strength Level defines five levels as population percentiles of its user-submitted e1RM database (Beginner = stronger than 5% of lifters, Novice 20%, Intermediate 50%, Advanced 80%, Elite 95%) and publishes per-exercise tables in 5 kg bodyweight rows that can be linearly interpolated (70 kg and 75 kg rows captured below for 13 exercises). Symmetric Strength does not use tables at all: each lift is converted to an implied powerlifting total via fixed lift ratios, scored as Wilks/4 (age-adjusted), and mapped to eight named tiers at fixed score cut-offs (30/45/60/75/87.5/100/112.5/125). ExRx uses Lon Kilgore's older, non-regression "performance standards" (Untrained/Novice/Intermediate/Advanced/Elite) in weight-class rows for four barbell lifts only.

### Inferences
- Linear interpolation of Strength Level's rows to 73 kg (computed, t = 0.6 between the 70 and 75 rows; consistent with the site's own statement that required weight "changed linearly with bodyweight" at a fixed level): bench 49.4 / 67.6 / 89.2 / 114.2 / 140.8; squat 66.2 / 90.8 / 120.8 / 155.0 / 191.6; deadlift 79.2 / 107.8 / 142.4 / 181.6 / 223.2; OHP 28.8 / 41.4 / 56.4 / 74.4 / 93.6; pull-up added 1RM -2 / +13.6 / +31.6 / +51.2 / +72.2; dip added 1RM +2.6 / +23.8 / +47.8 / +75.4 / +104; incline DB (per hand) 19.2 / 26.2 / 35.8 / 46.8 / 57.8; bent-over row 42.4 / 59.0 / 80.6 / 104.6 / 131.2; barbell curl 19.2 / 29.8 / 42.8 / 58.8 / 75.8; sled leg press 96 / 146.2 / 210.4 / 286.6 / 369.8; lying tricep extension 17.2 / 26.8 / 39.8 / 54.4 / 70.4.
- Strength Level's headline "bodyweight ratio" summaries are only approximate relative to the tables: at 73 kg the interpolated bench thresholds are 0.68x / 0.93x / 1.22x / 1.56x / 1.93x of bodyweight (computed), versus the summary's 0.5x / 1.0x / 1.25x / 1.5x / 2.0x. An implementer should store table rows, not the ratio summary.
- The "four coefficients ... continuous two-variable model, including a bodyweight-by-level interaction" is consistent with a model of the form W(bw, L) = b0 + b1*bw + b2*L + b3*bw*L (level L on a 1-5 scale), which can be inverted to a decimal level L = (W - b0 - b1*bw) / (b2 + b3*bw). This is my reading of the sentence, not a published formula.
- ExRx rows are weight classes (52, 56, 60, 67, 75, 82, 90 ... match historical IPF class limits), so a 73 kg lifter would normally read the 75 kg row; interpolating between 67 and 75 rows (computed) gives bench 83.1 kg Intermediate at 73 kg. ExRx's "Intermediate" (85 kg at 75 kg) is far below Strength Level's 50th-percentile "Intermediate" (92 kg at 75 kg) for bench but similar for the deadlift (135 vs 146), so the two systems are not interchangeable.
- Symmetric Strength's tiers are much harsher than Strength Level's names suggest (its FAQ says people training "a couple years" are often still "novice"); a score of 60 ("Intermediate") corresponds to a Wilks of 240 (computed: 60 x 4).

### Gaps
- Strength Level does not publish the actual regression coefficients, the percentile-to-level mapping between the five anchors, or the bodyweight assumed by its "By Age" tables.
- Strength Level's "bodyweight ratio" summary for pull-ups, chin-ups and dips was not shown on the fetched pages (only added-weight and rep tables).
- Symmetric Strength's standards page is rendered client-side (Angular) and its per-bodyweight tables could not be scraped; the tier cut-offs above come from its served JS, not from an HTML table.
- ExRx's Power Clean / Power Snatch tables and its lb tables were not fetched.

## Q2. How are weighted bodyweight exercises and dumbbell exercises standardised?

### Takeaway
Strength Level standardises pull-ups/chin-ups/dips as *added* weight (negative numbers mean assistance) and publishes separate rep tables; dumbbell standards are per single dumbbell and include the ~2 kg bar. Symmetric Strength takes the *total* load (bodyweight + added weight), converts the added-weight part (in lb) through a fitted 4th-order polynomial to a fraction of an implied powerlifting total, and caps reps at 10; it excludes dumbbell lifts entirely.

### Cited Findings
- Strength Level pull-up/chin-up/dip pages: "If the standard is negative, you have assistance weight. If positive, you add on weight using a weight belt." — [Pull-ups](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/pull-ups/kg); [Dips](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/dips/kg); [Chin-ups](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/chin-ups/kg)
- Strength Level FAQ on relative strength: "A lifter is considered stronger if they can lift the same total weight at a lower bodyweight, which demonstrates greater relative strength." — [FAQ](https://strengthlevel.com/faq)
- Strength Level dumbbells: "Enter the weight of the dumbbell in one hand, not the combined weight of both hands" and enter reps per arm without doubling. — [FAQ](https://strengthlevel.com/faq); incline DB page: "Dumbbell weights are for one dumbbell and include the weight of the bar, normally 2 kg / 4.4 lb". — [Incline DB bench](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/incline-dumbbell-bench-press/kg)
- Symmetric Strength bodyweight lifts: reps are capped at 10 and users are told "If you can do more than 10 pull-ups or dips, then try adding some weight using either a dumbbell between your legs or a dip belt. Then, next time you enter your lift, select the 'weighted' option." Also: bodyweight entered "is intended to be the weight at which your lifts were performed, rather than your competition weight. If you cut weight for a lifting competition and enter that weight in Symmetric Strength, your score will be inflated higher than it actually is." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Symmetric Strength code: for Dip/Chin-up/Pull-up the routine computes `l = (entered weight - bodyweight)` converted to lb, i.e. the entered value is the total load and `l` is the added weight; the fraction of the implied PL total is a polynomial in `l`:
  - Male Dip: 1.68064e-10*l^4 - 1.2946e-7*l^3 + 3.71905e-5*l^2 - 0.00499168*l + 0.566576
  - Female Dip: 8.249e-10*l^4 - 4.01956e-7*l^3 + 6.22122e-5*l^2 - 0.00431442*l + 0.37562
  - Male Chin-up: 4.01897e-10*l^4 - 2.34536e-7*l^3 + 5.02252e-5*l^2 - 0.00502633*l + 0.459545
  - Female Chin-up: 1.66589e-9*l^4 - 5.1621e-7*l^3 + 5.4088e-5*l^2 - 0.00281674*l + 0.302005
  - Pull-up = 0.95 x Chin-up fraction. The functions `liftIsSpecial` (dip, chinup, pullup) and `liftFromStrengthScoreSpecialHelper` (iterative solve, up to 50 iterations, tolerance 1%) exist because the fraction depends on the load itself. — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)
- Open-source example (Flask): "Pull-ups, chin-ups, dips, ring dips: bodyweight + optional added weight + reps" with "total weight lifted ... calculated by adding external weight to bodyweight"; its thresholds are self-described as "reasonable estimates, not authoritative data". — [nazirhusyan23-create/strength-standards-calculator](https://github.com/nazirhusyan23-create/strength-standards-calculator)
- Open-source example (spreadsheet): "For chin-ups, pull-ups, and dips, users enter added weight (negative if assisted), then calculate 1RM via Wathan's formula." — [danmcne/Body-Assessment](https://github.com/danmcne/Body-Assessment)

### Inferences
- Both major systems agree on the physics: the quantity that is bodyweight-normalised is total system load (bodyweight + added), while the UI shows added weight. For an app: store `added_kg` (may be negative for assisted), compute `total = bodyweight + added` for scaling/e1RM, and display `added`.
- Because Strength Level's pull-up 1RM thresholds are almost flat across bodyweight (70 kg: +31 Intermediate; 75 kg: +32), scaling weighted pull-ups by a simple ratio of *added* weight to bodyweight would be wrong; total-load ratios (e.g. (73+31.6)/73 = 1.43x bodyweight for Intermediate, computed) behave sensibly.
- Worked example of the Symmetric Strength dip model (computed from the code, 73 kg male, +40 kg dip = 113 kg total; l = 88.18 lb): polynomial = 0.3370, implied PL total = 113/0.3370 = 335.3 kg, Wilks = 335.3 x 0.7264 = 243.6, score = 60.9 -> "Intermediate". For a +30 kg chin-up (103 kg total, l = 66.14 lb): fraction 0.2866, implied total 359.3 kg, Wilks 261.0, score 65.3 -> "Intermediate".
- Dumbbell standards should be keyed per hand (Strength Level convention) and any per-exercise ratio table should say so explicitly; Symmetric Strength's decision to exclude dumbbells suggests dumbbell 1RM data are noisy.

### Gaps
- Strength Level does not state whether its dumbbell tables' e1RM is estimated from per-arm reps identically to barbell lifts.
- No source documents how Hevy, Liftoff or Fitbod treat added weight on pull-ups/dips.

## Q3. How does Symmetric Strength aggregate lifts into muscle-group scores and an overall "symmetry" picture?

### Takeaway
Symmetric Strength scores each lift as (age factor x Wilks of the implied powerlifting total)/4, takes the *best* score in each of five movement categories (squat, floor pull, pull-up, horizontal press, vertical press) and averages them for the overall score, computes each of 22 muscle groups as a weighted average of lift scores using a published per-lift involvement table (weights 0-10, cubed in the average), and defines symmetry = 100 minus the variance of lift scores.

### Cited Findings
- Overall score: "The 'total' strength score averages the highest scores in each category: The squat, floor pull, pull-up, horizontal press, and vertical press." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Why best-per-category: "The reason Symmetric Strength uses the best lifts in each category rather than just averaging every lift entered is because of muscle group overlap. For example, if somebody checked four lifts - the Bench, Incline Bench, Dip, and Deadlift - and was elite in the first three and intermediate in the deadlift, then all we can conclude is that the lifter is incredibly strong at horizontal pressing and comparitively weak in the floor pull. We wouldn't want to count the horizontal press three times." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Muscle groups: "The score for a single muscle group is measured by a weighted average of the lifts which involve that muscle group." and "If Symmetric Strength does not have enough data to determine the strength of a muscle group, the muscle group will remain grey." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Symmetry: "The symmetry score is a measure of how much the strength levels among your lifts vary. ... 100 is the highest possible score, and indicates that all your lifts are equal to those of an average lifter at your strength level. Negative scores are possible and indicate pronounced asymmetry. Higher scores are not necessarily better than lower scores, since some lifters might naturally be stronger on certain lifts due to their builds, leverages, or other non training-related factors." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Relative strengths/weaknesses chart: "Your relative scores are comparing you with other lifters that are at your same overall score ... If you only enter one lift per category (except the 'Miscellaneous' category), the percentages are guaranteed to add up to 0%, give or take 1% due to rounding." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Code, weighted average: `weightedAvg(pairs)` = sum(score_i x w_i^3) / sum(w_i^3), returned only if sum(w_i^3) >= 50, else 0 (i.e. the muscle stays grey). Symmetry: `symmetryScore(scores) = 100 - population variance of the scores`. — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)
- Code, pipeline: `expectedPLTotal = lift / percentOfPLTotal`; `expectedWilks = wilks(bodyweight, expectedPLTotal)`; `wilksToStrengthScore(wilks, age) = wilks x ageFactor / 4`; `singleLiftStrengthScore` chains them. — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)

### Inferences
- Category membership is not spelled out in the fetched text; from the lift list and the FAQ example it is evidently: squat = back/front squat; floor pull = deadlift/sumo/power clean (and probably Pendlay row); horizontal press = bench/incline/dip; vertical press = OHP/push press/snatch press; pull-up = chin-up/pull-up; plus a "Miscellaneous" category mentioned in the FAQ.
- Cubing the involvement weights (w^3) makes a muscle's score dominated by the lifts that load it most (a weight-10 lift counts 1000 vs 8 for weight 2), and the sum(w^3) >= 50 rule means a muscle needs at least one lift with involvement >= 4 (4^3 = 64) before it is scored.
- Worked overall score (computed with the code above, 73 kg male aged 25-40, Wilks coefficient 0.7264): bench 90 kg -> implied total 348.9 kg -> score 63.4 (Intermediate); squat 120 kg -> 63.1; deadlift 140 kg -> 64.1; OHP 55 kg -> 59.6 (Novice); +30 kg chin-up -> 65.3. Overall = mean of the best per category = (63.1 + 64.1 + 65.3 + 63.4 + 59.6)/5 = 63.1 (Intermediate); symmetry = 100 - variance = 100 - 3.7 = 96.3.
- A personal app can copy the architecture directly: (1) per-lift normalised score, (2) best-per-movement-pattern to avoid double counting, (3) per-muscle weighted average with an involvement matrix, (4) a dispersion statistic for balance.

## Q4. Which bodyweight-normalisation coefficients exist (Wilks, DOTS, IPF GL, Sinclair, allometric), what are the exact formulas, and does height ever enter?

### Takeaway
All federation formulas take bodyweight and sex only: Wilks (5th-degree polynomial, 500 numerator; 2020 revision uses 600), DOTS (4th-degree polynomial, 500 numerator), IPF GL (exponential, 100 numerator, separate SBD/bench and raw/equipped parameter sets) and Sinclair (10^(A*(log10(bw/b))^2)). Allometric scaling divides load by bodyweight^k, with k = 2/3 by theory but empirically 0.45-0.68 depending on population (0.55 for the general powerlifting population in a 2026 study of 457,471 lifters). Height appears in none of them; the only evidence-based route to use height is via fat-free mass (Folland 2008 found height still biases mass-normalised strength but not FFM-normalised strength).

### Cited Findings

**Wilks (original)**
- Coeff = 500 / (a + b*x + c*x^2 + d*x^3 + e*x^4 + f*x^5), x = bodyweight kg. Men: a = -216.0475144, b = 16.2606339, c = -0.002388645, d = -0.00113732, e = 7.01863e-06, f = -1.291e-08. Women: a = 594.31747775582, b = -27.23842536447, c = 0.82112226871, d = -0.00930733913, e = 4.731582e-05, f = -9.054e-08. — [Wikipedia: Wilks coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilks_coefficient); identical constants in [OpenPowerlifting wilks.rs](https://gitlab.com/openpowerlifting/opl-data/-/raw/main/crates/coefficients/src/wilks.rs) and in [Symmetric Strength libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)
- OpenPowerlifting clamps bodyweight to 40.0-201.9 kg (men) and 26.51-154.53 kg (women) to avoid polynomial "asymptote" problems and "children with huge coefficients". — [wilks.rs](https://gitlab.com/openpowerlifting/opl-data/-/raw/main/crates/coefficients/src/wilks.rs)

**Wilks 2020**
- Coeff = 600 / (a + b*x + ... + f*x^5). Men: a = 47.46178854, b = 8.472061379, c = 0.07369410346, d = -0.001395833811, e = 7.07665973070743e-06, f = -1.20804336482315e-08. Women: a = -125.4255398, b = 13.71219419, c = -0.03307250631, d = -0.001050400051, e = 9.38773881462799e-06, f = -2.3334613884954e-08. — [Wikipedia: Wilks coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilks_coefficient)

**DOTS**
- Points = Total x 500 / (A*x^4 + B*x^3 + C*x^2 + D*x + E). Men: A = -0.0000010930, B = 0.0007391293, C = -0.1918759221, D = 24.0900756, E = -307.75076, bodyweight clamped to 40.0-210.0 kg. Women: A = -0.0000010706, B = 0.0005158568, C = -0.1126655495, D = 13.6175032, E = -57.96288, clamped 40.0-150.0 kg. Origin: developed by Tim Konertz for the German IPF affiliate BVDK; "Dynamic Objective Team Scoring". — [OpenPowerlifting dots.rs](https://gitlab.com/openpowerlifting/opl-data/-/raw/main/crates/coefficients/src/dots.rs)

**IPF GL (Goodlift) points**
- Points = Total x 100 / (A - B * e^(-C * bodyweight)), zero if bodyweight < 35 kg; "The formula was made for the IPF, it only covers Raw and Single-ply." Parameters: SBD Male Raw A = 1199.72839, B = 1025.18162, C = 0.009210; SBD Male Equipped A = 1236.25115, B = 1449.21864, C = 0.01644; SBD Female Raw A = 610.32796, B = 1045.59282, C = 0.03048; SBD Female Equipped A = 758.63878, B = 949.31382, C = 0.02435; Bench-only Male Raw A = 320.98041, B = 281.40258, C = 0.01008; Bench Male Equipped A = 381.22073, B = 733.79378, C = 0.02398; Bench Female Raw A = 142.40398, B = 442.52671, C = 0.04724; Bench Female Equipped A = 221.82209, B = 357.00377, C = 0.02937. — [OpenPowerlifting goodlift.rs](https://gitlab.com/openpowerlifting/opl-data/-/raw/main/crates/coefficients/src/goodlift.rs)

**Sinclair (Olympic weightlifting)**
- Coefficient = 10^(A x (log10(x/b))^2) if x < b, else 1.0, where b is "the world record holder's bodyweight in the heaviest category". 2017-2020: Men A = 0.751945030, b = 175.508 kg; Women A = 0.783497476, b = 153.655 kg. — [Wikipedia: Sinclair coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_coefficient)
- 2021-2024 (described as "the most recent set the International Weightlifting Federation has published in full"): Men A = 0.722762521, b = 193.609 kg; Women A = 0.787004341, b = 153.757 kg. "Because the weight categories were revised in mid-2025, the 2025 to 2028 constants are still being recalibrated on the new records". — [Coachway Sinclair calculator](https://coachway.io/tools/sinclair-calculator/) (secondary source; the IWF site returned 403)

**Allometric scaling**
- Theory: "because strength is proportional to muscle cross-sectional area and body mass is proportional to volume, isometric scaling (or the square-cube law with a 2/3 exponent) offered an intellectually pleasing theoretical model." Lietzke (1956) showed it on weightlifting records "though it failed for heavier athletes"; "Austin proposed an updated allometric model with a 3/4 exponent"; Vanderburgh recommended "BP x BM^-0.57 and SQ x BM^-0.60"; Nuckols: a log-log plot of size vs strength has "a slope around 2/3". — [Taylor's Strength: powerlifting formulas](https://www.taylorsstrength.co.uk/powerlifting-formulas-is-wilks-best-and-what-are-the-alternatives/)
- Stronger by Science: "I'm very fond of allometric scaling for normalizing strength to body size as opposed to more commonly used methods such as strength to bodyweight ratios or Wilks scores"; its explainer says 2/3-power scaling "does a very good job of normalizing strength performances among elite competitors" and beats bodyweight ratios. — [SBS: What is strong?](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/how-to-get-strong-what-is-strong/); [SBS search summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/whos-the-most-impressive-powerlifter/) (the latter page returned 404 when fetched directly)
- Folland, Mc Cauley & Williams 2008 (Eur J Appl Physiol; 86 young men, knee extensor force/torque): "To normalise strength measurements to body mass, for relatively homogenous lean populations (body fat <20%), exponents of 0.66 (F) and 1.0 (T) are appropriate. For more adipose populations (body fat >20%) lower body mass exponents appear more suitable (F, 0.45; T, 0.68). Nevertheless, fat-free mass is the recommended index for scaling strength to body size, and higher exponents (F, 0.76; T, 1.12) are advocated in this case." and "Absolute force and torque measurements, and even torque normalised for body mass, were significantly influenced by height, although strength measures normalised to fat-free mass were not." — [Europe PMC record for PMID 18172672](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:18172672&resultType=core&format=json)
- Montenegro, Wicker & Donath 2026 (Frontiers in Physiology; 457,471 powerlifters): empirically derived allometric exponents "decay" toward elite status — general population males b = 0.55, females b = 0.50; elite Top-20 males b = 0.47, females b = 0.41; "The empirical allometric model achieved equivalence with the IPF GL formula for elite males." — [Europe PMC search result](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22allometric%20modeling%22%20AND%20%22bench%20press%22%20AND%20squat&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=5)
- Fahey 2026 (Eur J Appl Physiol 126(8)): sex-specific log-log models "ln(P) = ln(a) + b*ln(M)" for bench and squat with regression-residual normalisation reduced the body-mass/performance correlation to r = -0.06 to 0.01 (males). Vanderburgh & Dooman 2000 (MSSE 32(1)): a second-order polynomial fitted women's records with no mass bias better than allometric models, "though allometric modeling performed adequately for bench press". — [same Europe PMC search](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22allometric%20modeling%22%20AND%20%22bench%20press%22%20AND%20squat&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=5)
- Symmetric Strength's own caveat: "Though the strength score adjusts by weight, it does not do so linearly. For example, it is much easier for a light lifter to hit a double-bodyweight front squat than it is for a heavy lifter." — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)

**Height**
- A small study of 11 powerlifters reported lean body mass correlations with squat (r = 0.899), bench (r = 0.915), deadlift (r = 0.901) and total (r = 0.923) at baseline, but "No correlations involving height with strength measures were reported." — [PMC11130886](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11130886/)
- Anatomical caveat on scaling: "An isometric scaling relationship assumes that all lifters are perfect geometric copies of each other, scaled to different sizes, which they obviously aren't." — [Taylor's Strength](https://www.taylorsstrength.co.uk/powerlifting-formulas-is-wilks-best-and-what-are-the-alternatives/)
- Stronger by Science mentions height only as "kilos of FFM per cm of height" (~0.32 kg/cm men vs ~0.22 kg/cm women). — [SBS: What is strong?](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/how-to-get-strong-what-is-strong/)
- Symmetric Strength uses height only in its ideal-bodyweight calculators, not in strength scoring; e.g. `idealWeightMaleMaximumMuscularPotential`: lean = 0.9*height_cm - 80, minus 5.5%, divided by (1 - bodyfat); and its FFMI reference: "having an FFMI of 19.2 would place an 18-34 year old woman in the 99.9th percentile" (citing "Fat-free mass index and fat mass index percentiles in Caucasians aged 18-98 y"). — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js); [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)

### Inferences
- Computed coefficients for the target lifter (male, 73 kg): Wilks polynomial = 688.37 -> coefficient 0.7264 (Wilks = total x 0.7264); DOTS denominator = 684.81 -> coefficient 0.7301; IPF GL raw SBD denominator = 1199.72839 - 1025.18162 x e^(-0.6723) = 676.4 -> a 350 kg total = 51.7 GL points, 255.5 DOTS, 254.2 Wilks; 73^(2/3) = 17.47, so a 90 kg bench = 5.15 allometric units.
- For a general gym population (not elite), the best-supported single exponent is around 0.55-0.66 (Montenegro 2026 general-population 0.55; Folland lean 0.66; Vanderburgh 0.57 bench / 0.60 squat). The classic 2/3 is a fine default; 1.0 (plain bodyweight ratio) systematically penalises heavier and flatters lighter lifters.
- Height should not be added as a separate term to a bodyweight-scaled standard: no published standard does it, and the one controlled study found the height bias disappears once strength is normalised to fat-free mass. The evidence-based way to "use" 179 cm is indirectly: estimate FFM (bodyweight x (1 - bodyfat)) and scale by FFM^0.76 (Folland), or use height only for body-composition context (FFMI-style), never as a strength multiplier.

### Gaps
- The 2025-2028 Sinclair constants could not be verified from the IWF (403); only the secondary claim that they were still being recalibrated as of the fetched calculator page.
- Wilks-2020 bodyweight validity ranges are not stated on Wikipedia.
- No study was found that publishes a height-adjusted strength standard or a limb-length correction for the deadlift/bench.

## Q5. How do these systems handle age and sex adjustments, and estimated vs tested 1RM?

### Takeaway
Sex is always a separate table/coefficient set. Age is either a separate table (Strength Level: baseline ages 25-40, lower thresholds outside it) or a multiplier on the score (Symmetric Strength: quadratic fits to the Foster (<23) and McCulloch (>40) powerlifting age coefficients; OpenPowerlifting: the same tables applied to DOTS/Wilks). Every app scores an *estimated* 1RM from logged sets, but with different formulas: Strength Level (Brzycki <8 reps, Epley >10, interpolated 8-10), Symmetric Strength (Wathan, max 10 reps), Hevy (fixed percentage table, 50% floor above 30 reps), liftmath (median of six formulas), Stronger/Flask calculator (Epley).

### Cited Findings
- Strength Level: "The current model uses ages 25-40 as its adult baseline and adjusts the standards for lifters younger than 25 or older than 40." and "Separate male and female standards are applied across all exercises." — [About](https://strengthlevel.com/about)
- Strength Level 1RM: "For fewer than 8 repetitions, we use the Brzycki formula"; "For more than 10 repetitions, we use the Epley formula"; "For repetitions between 8 and 10, we use a linear interpolation of the Brzycki and Epley formulas". Lifters "could enter working sets of one to ten repetitions. We converted each set to an estimated one-rep maximum (1RM) and used those results to build standards". — [FAQ](https://strengthlevel.com/faq); [About](https://strengthlevel.com/about)
- Symmetric Strength age adjustment: "The age adjustment formulas used by Symmetric Strength are quadratic fits to the revised McCulloch coefficients (for lifters over 40 years old) and the Foster coefficients (for lifters under 23 years old)." Sources listed: Foster Age Coefficients http://www.usapl-sd.com/Formulas/Foster.htm and McCulloch Revised Master's Coefficients http://globalpowerliftingalliance.com/master%20age%20formula.html. — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Symmetric Strength age code (`wilksToStrengthScore`): factor t = 1; if age < 23: t = 0.0038961*age^2 - 0.166926*age + 2.80303; if age > 40: t = 0.000467683*age^2 - 0.0299717*age + 1.45454; score = wilks x t / 4. — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)

### Inferences
- Implied Strength Level age multipliers from its bench "By Age" rows (computed, ratio to the 25-40 row 49/70/96/127/160): age 20 = 0.96-0.98, age 45 = 0.94-0.95, age 50 = 0.88-0.89, roughly the inverse of the McCulloch multipliers at 45 (1.055) and 50 (1.130). An app can therefore apply McCulloch/Foster multipliers to the lifter's e1RM (or divide thresholds by them) and get results close to both systems.
- Symmetric Strength's quadratics reproduce the tables well (computed: t(50) = 1.125 vs McCulloch 1.130; t(60) = 1.340 vs 1.340; t(20) = 1.023 vs Foster 1.03; t(18) = 1.061 vs 1.06), so a quadratic is an acceptable compact encoding; for ages 23-40 the factor is exactly 1.
- Tested vs estimated 1RM: none of the consumer systems distinguishes them; Symmetric Strength and Strength Level both refuse or down-weight high-rep sets (10-rep cap; formula switching), which is the practical mitigation. Storing the rep count that produced each e1RM and preferring sets of <= 5-6 reps for ranking is consistent with all sources.

### Gaps
- Strength Level's exact age curves (per age year) and the Brzycki/Epley formulas were not quoted on its pages (the FAQ names them without the formula text); Brzycki's standard form (w x 36/(37 - reps)) was not verified from a fetched source in this session.
- Hevy does not publish its age groups or bodyweight bands.

### Inferences
- A personal (non-distributed) app that types in a handful of Strength Level rows for private use is low-risk, but shipping those tables or a lookalike calculator would breach Strength Level's terms; ExRx tables need written permission for anything beyond private use.
- The clean-room route is: (a) derive squat/bench/deadlift percentile thresholds from the CC0 OpenPowerlifting CSV (as liftmath does with DOTS buckets), (b) use public formulas (Wilks/DOTS/IPF GL, allometric exponent) with the federation-published coefficients, (c) express accessory-lift standards as ratios to the big lifts (Symmetric Strength's published ratios 0.65 bench:deadlift, 0.87 squat:deadlift, 0.65 OHP:bench, 0.82 incline:bench, 0.53 Pendlay row:deadlift are the only openly documented set), and (d) label everything as estimates.
- Note that OpenPowerlifting-derived percentiles describe competitive powerlifters, so "50th percentile" there is far stronger than Strength Level's gym-population "Intermediate" (Barbell Medicine: 1-2 year recreational lifters sit near the 10th percentile of competitors).

### Gaps
- No open dataset of gym-population (non-competition) strength standards was found; Strength Level's is the only large one and it is closed.
- The exact licence of Symmetric Strength's served code and whether it permits reuse is unknown (no terms page found).

## Q7. How do apps present the rank (badges, percentiles, radar/body charts)?

### Takeaway
Presentation converges on: a named tier per exercise, a continuous score or percentile ("stronger than X%"), and a per-muscle colour-coded body diagram for aggregation (Symmetric Strength, Stronger, Fitbod), with gamified badge ladders (Liftoff's Wood-to-Olympian) and single 0-100 composite numbers (Boostcamp, Fitbod) as the two alternative summaries.

### Cited Findings
- Strength Level: per exercise it shows the level plus "stronger than X% of lifters", tables "By Bodyweight" and "By Age", and the calculator "return[s] a decimal rating ... including ratings between levels on the original Untrained (1) to Elite (5) scale". — [Bench page](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/bench-press/kg); [About](https://strengthlevel.com/about)
- Symmetric Strength: colour-coded tiers (hex colours in Q1), a "relative strengths and weaknesses chart" in percentages relative to lifters at the same overall score, muscle groups drawn on a body figure that "will remain grey" when data are insufficient, an overall score and a symmetry score. — [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about); [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js)
- Hevy: "compare your overall strength in specific exercises with other users of the same sex, age group, and bodyweight. It will also let you know what percentage of people in this group you are stronger than"; levels "Beginner Intermediate Advanced Elite"; located under Profile > Exercises > exercise > "Strength Level"; available for 13 exercises: Behind the Back Bicep Wrist Curl (Barbell), Bench Press (Barbell), Bicep Curl (Barbell), Deadlift (Barbell), Hip Thrust (Barbell), Leg Press (Machine), Preacher Curl (Barbell), Reverse Curl (Barbell), Romanian Deadlift (Barbell), Seated Wrist Extension (Barbell), Skullcrusher (Barbell), Spider Curl (Barbell), Squat (Barbell). — [Hevy help](https://help.hevyapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/38001696749335-Strength-Level-Feature-How-Hevy-Compares-Your-Strength-by-Exercise)
- Liftoff: "1 to 1000 strength rating that accounts for your bodyweight and sex, mapped to nine ranks from Wood to Olympian" (Wood, Iron, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Diamond, Champion, Olympian); "Each set is scored based on an estimated one rep max"; "Watch ranks climb per exercise, per muscle, and overall"; "Strength ranks cover 400 of the 600 exercises"; leaderboards by friends, country and world; XP, quests, streaks, medals, cosmetic shop. FAQ: "Ranks are calculated using your bodyweight and your 1RM"; "Lift Points (LP) is the new points system for ranks. Each rank has a range from 0 to 100 LP and a rank up happens every 100 LP, except for the Olympian rank."; "Your Liftoff Rank is calculated based on an accumulated score of your exercise ranks. The higher the exercise score, the more it counts towards your Liftoff Rank." — [Liftoff gamified tracker](https://liftoffrank.com/gamified-workout-tracker); [Liftoff FAQ](https://liftoffrank.com/en/faq)
- Fitbod: per-muscle "Muscle Strength" score "on a scale of 0-100+, where a score of 50 is what an average Fitbod user can lift" ("1.67% of all weekly strength score data points are above 100"); scores are relative per muscle ("having a higher score in the biceps than the quadriceps does not mean your biceps can lift more weight than your quads"); shown in the Workout Report with line graphs; an "Overall Strength Score" is "a single number" that "represents your overall strength fitness level" — no formula published. — [Fitbod Muscle Strength](https://fitbod.me/blog/muscle-strength/); [Fitbod metrics blog](https://fitbod.me/blog/how-fitbod-tracks-your-strength-progress-with-real-time-metrics-and-scores/)
- Boostcamp (Pro only): "a single 0-100 number" using "the IPF DOTS formula" across "squat, bench, deadlift, overhead press, and rows", computed from lifetime PRs; shown alongside a per-muscle volume heatmap. — [Boostcamp features](https://www.boostcamp.app/features); [Boostcamp Pro](https://www.boostcamp.app/pro)
- Stronger app: six levels "Beginner, Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Elite, World Class"; "Each lift you enter is scored from Beginner to World Class, and the corresponding muscle group on the body diagram lights up with the matching colour"; "When you have scored three or more muscle groups, the calculator displays your overall Strength Score — the average of all your scored muscle groups." Its blog reuses Strength Level's five percentile tiers (5/20/50/80/95) and the 5 kg-row table approach because "strength does not scale linearly with bodyweight". — [Stronger strength score](https://www.strongermobileapp.com/strength-score); [Stronger blog](https://www.strongermobileapp.com/blog/strength-standards)
- Alpha Progression: its guide describes estimated 1RM/10RM, PR tracking, strength/volume charts, streaks and achievement badges, but "No strength-standards or comparative ranking feature is described". — [Alpha Progression guide](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/alpha-progression-guide)
- Strong app: "Strong will log your lifts but does not tell you how they stand relative to strength standards." — [Stronger vs Strong comparison](https://www.strongermobileapp.com/compare/strong) (competitor-authored, treat as claim)
- Barbell Medicine presents standards as decile charts from competition data and warns they are "what populations have achieved", "descriptive orientation tools", not targets. — [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-standards/)

### Inferences
- Reviewer and store comments on Liftoff (via search summary) that standards are "very off" for lesser-used exercises and "don't account for weight class" illustrate the main risk of ranking hundreds of accessory lifts: thin data. Limiting ranked exercises to well-populated ones (Hevy: 13; Symmetric Strength: 14) is the conservative choice.
- A per-body-part radar chart was not documented by any source; the documented visual idiom is a colour-coded body/muscle diagram plus per-exercise tier labels and a percentile sentence.

### Gaps
- Boostcamp does not document how DOTS is mapped onto 0-100, nor how the five lifts are combined (sum of PRs vs per-lift averaging).
- Liftoff's mapping from the 1-1000 rating to rank cut-offs and its per-muscle formula are not published.

## Q8. Implementation blueprint for a 179 cm / 73 kg male lifter (synthesis)

### Takeaway
A defensible personal implementation combines (1) an e1RM per exercise from low-rep sets, (2) bodyweight normalisation by either interpolated per-exercise tables (Strength Level style) or a formula (Wilks/DOTS/allometric with k about 0.55-0.67), (3) exercise-to-muscle mapping with best-lift-per-movement-pattern de-duplication (Symmetric Strength style), and (4) a five-tier label plus continuous score; height is used only for body-composition context.

### Cited Findings
- Strength Level's model is linear in bodyweight at fixed level (Q1), so table rows can be linearly interpolated. — [About](https://strengthlevel.com/about)
- Symmetric Strength's full pipeline (ratios, Wathan, Wilks/4, age quadratics, involvement matrix, cubic weighting, best-per-category mean, 100 - variance symmetry) is in Q1-Q5 above. — [libs.js](https://symmetricstrength.com/static/res/libs.js); [About](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)
- Barbell Medicine recreational expectations after 1-2 years: squat 1.0-1.5x, bench 0.7-1.1x, deadlift 1.25-1.75x bodyweight (roughly the 10th percentile of competitive powerlifters); competitor 90th percentile male: squat 2.83x, deadlift 3.25x, bench 1.95x. — [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-standards/)
- Barbell Medicine's "strong enough for health" leg-press benchmark is calibrated to the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (8,762 men, 18.9-year follow-up; moving from the weakest to the middle third of strength was associated with 28% lower all-cause mortality); a search snippet of the same article states "if you can bench press 1.1 times your bodyweight and leg press 1.9 times your bodyweight, you are strong enough for health" (snippet text; not confirmed verbatim in the fetched page). — [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-standards/)

### Inferences (worked design, all numbers computed from the cited sources)
1. **e1RM**: use Epley for <= 10 reps (w x (1 + r/30)) or Wathan (100w / (48.8 + 53.8 e^(-0.075 r))); ignore sets > 10 reps for ranking (Symmetric Strength's cap) or switch to Hevy's percentage table if you want a defined behaviour up to 30 reps. Example: 80 kg x 5 -> Epley 93.3 kg, Wathan 93.6 kg, Hevy 89.9 kg.
2. **Normalisation options for 73 kg**: (a) table interpolation (Strength Level rows in Q1: bench 89.2 kg = Intermediate line at 73 kg); (b) Wilks coefficient 0.7264, DOTS 0.7301, IPF GL denominator 676.4; (c) allometric: divide by 73^0.67 = 17.7 (or 73^0.55 = 10.6 for a general-population exponent). Any of these can be turned into a level by comparing with thresholds expressed in the same units.
3. **Level labels**: five tiers with Strength Level's percentile semantics (5/20/50/80/95) are the most widely copied (Stronger, Hevy's four-tier variant); Symmetric Strength's eight tiers at score 30/45/60/75/87.5/100/112.5/125 are the alternative when you score in Wilks/4 units.
4. **Accessory and bodyweight lifts**: derive thresholds as ratios of the big-lift thresholds (OHP = 0.65 x bench, incline = 0.82 x bench, Pendlay row = 0.53 x deadlift, front squat = 0.8 x back squat, per Symmetric Strength) or take Strength Level's per-exercise rows; for pull-ups/dips normalise total load (bodyweight + added) and display added load (Strength Level 73 kg Intermediate lines: pull-up about +31.6 kg, dip about +47.8 kg; Symmetric Strength via its polynomials).
5. **Per body part**: map each exercise to muscle groups with 0-10 involvement weights (Symmetric Strength's matrix in Q3 covers 14 barbell/bodyweight lifts; extend for curls, tricep extensions, leg press yourself), score = sum(score x w^3)/sum(w^3), require sum(w^3) >= 50 before showing a muscle, and use the best lift per movement pattern (squat, hinge, horizontal push, vertical push, vertical pull, horizontal pull, elbow flexion, elbow extension) before averaging into an overall score; report balance as 100 - variance or as 100 x geometric/arithmetic mean.
6. **Age/sex**: male tables; apply factor 1.0 at ages 23-40, McCulloch/Foster multipliers or Symmetric Strength's quadratics outside that range.
7. **Height (179 cm)**: do not add a height term to the strength score (no evidence base); optionally show FFM-based context (FFM = 73 x (1 - bodyfat); Folland recommends scaling force by FFM^0.76) or FFMI, and keep height out of the tier calculation.
8. **Presentation**: tier label + "stronger than ~X%" (interpolate linearly between the five percentile anchors, e.g. a 90 kg bench at 73 kg sits at about the 51st percentile on Strength Level's anchors) + colour-coded body diagram; keep the ranked-exercise list short.

### Gaps
- No source publishes gym-population tables for curls, tricep extensions, leg press or incline dumbbell press other than Strength Level (proprietary), so accessory-lift thresholds in a clean-room implementation will be estimates.
- The linear percentile interpolation between Strength Level's five anchors is my simplification; the true distribution between anchors is not published.
