# Exercise-science evidence for the defaults and calculations of a personal strength-training app

Scope: rest intervals, progression schemes, estimated-1RM formulas, weekly volume per muscle group, bodyweight/height normalisation, and body-part strength ratios. Metric units throughout. Reference user: 179 cm, 73 kg male, general strength + physique. Research date: 26 Sep 2026. Publication years are given for every source; "expert opinion" is flagged where the source is a coach/program author rather than peer-reviewed evidence.

Worked numbers for the reference user (73 kg): 73^0.67 = 17.7 (allometric divisor); 90th-percentile drug-tested powerlifter ratios (see Q5) would correspond to squat 207 kg, bench 142 kg, deadlift 237 kg; the median raw male in the 74 kg class on OpenPowerlifting lifts roughly squat 170 kg, bench 112.5 kg, deadlift 202 kg (see Q6).

## Q1. Rest intervals: what do meta-analyses/reviews recommend, and what do apps use as defaults?

### Takeaway
The modern evidence (Schoenfeld 2016 RCT; Grgic 2017/2018 reviews; Singer/Schoenfeld 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis) says: rest more than 60 s for hypertrophy with no detectable extra benefit beyond ~90 s, and rest 2+ min (3–5 min in position stands) when maximal strength on heavy compound lifts is the goal in trained lifters; the old "30–60 s for hypertrophy" advice (de Salles 2009, NSCA) is not supported. Popular apps use a single default (Strong: 2:00) and let the user override per exercise.

### Cited Findings
- Singer, Wolf, Generoso, ... Schoenfeld (2024), Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, Bayesian meta-analysis of 9 studies / 19 measurements (10 thigh, 6 arm, 3 whole body). Rest categories: short ≤60 s; intermediate 60–120 s; long 120–180 s; very long ≥180 s. Binary comparison (≤60 s vs >60 s) SMD favouring longer rest: arm 0.13 (95% CrI −0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (−0.13 to 0.43), whole body −0.08 (−0.45 to 0.29). Absolute SMDs: short rest 0.48 (0.19–0.81) vs longer rest 0.56 (0.24–0.86). Authors recommend resting >60 s and state the "analysis did not detect appreciable differences in hypertrophy when resting >90 s between sets"; they note this contradicts NSCA guidelines of 30–90 s for hypertrophy. Trained-participant data were insufficient for subgroup analysis; studies mixed free weights/machines and single-/multi-joint exercises. — [Singer et al. 2024, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- Grgic et al. (2017), European Journal of Sport Science, systematic review of 6 studies comparing short (≤60 s) vs long (>60 s) inter-set rest for hypertrophy: "both short and long inter-set rest intervals may be useful", with "a possible advantage for the use of long rest intervals" in trained participants. — [Grgic et al. 2017 (Europe PMC, PMID 28641044)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28641044)
- Grgic et al. (2018), Sports Medicine, systematic review on strength: "Robust gains in muscular strength can be achieved even with short RIs (<60 s). However, it seems that longer duration RIs (>2 min) are required to maximize strength gains in resistance-trained individuals. With regard to untrained individuals, it seems that short to moderate RIs (60–120 s) are sufficient for maximizing muscular strength gains." — [Grgic et al. 2018 (Europe PMC, PMID 28933024)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28933024)
- Schoenfeld et al. (2016), JSCR, RCT in 21 resistance-trained men: 1-min vs 3-min rest, 8 weeks, 3 sessions/week, 3 sets of 8–12 RM on 7 exercises. 1RM squat and bench press gains were significantly greater with 3 min; anterior-thigh muscle thickness significantly greater with 3 min; triceps trend (p = 0.06). Conclusion: "longer rest periods promote greater increases in muscle strength and hypertrophy in young resistance-trained men." — [Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (Europe PMC, PMID 26605807)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26605807)
- Henselmans & Schoenfeld (2014), Sports Medicine, review: "the literature does not support the hypothesis that training for muscle hypertrophy requires shorter rest intervals than training for strength development." — [Henselmans & Schoenfeld 2014 (Europe PMC, PMID 25047853)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25047853)
- de Salles et al. (2009), Sports Medicine (older review, partly superseded): 3–5 min between sets produced greater absolute-strength gains; for hypertrophy the review suggested moderate-intensity sets with 30–60 s rest (a hormonal-response argument later rejected by Henselmans & Schoenfeld 2014 and Singer 2024); endurance 20 s–1 min; power better maintained with 3 or 5 min vs 1 min. — [de Salles et al. 2009 (Europe PMC, PMID 19691365)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19691365)
- ACSM Position Stand (2009), MSSE: heavy loading (1–6 RM) for strength with "3- to 5-min rest periods"; hypertrophy in the "6–12 RM zone" with "1- to 2-min rest periods"; local muscular endurance (>15 reps at 40–60% 1RM) with "<90 s" rest; power training with 3–5 min rest. — [ACSM 2009 (Europe PMC, PMID 19204579)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19204579)
- Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland (2021), Sports Medicine, narrative review on time-efficient training: quotes common guidelines of "3–5 min rest interval when training to maximize strength, a 1–2 min rest interval when the goal is hypertrophy, and a 30–60 s rest interval when the goal is muscular endurance", then recommends 1–2 min for untrained and ≥2 min for trained individuals; also "minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group using a 6–15 RM loading range"; supersets/drop sets/rest-pause "roughly halves training time" while maintaining volume, probably better for hypertrophy than strength. — [Iversen et al. 2021 full text (Europe PMC, PMC8449772)](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC8449772); abstract at [PMID 34125411](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34125411)
- Renaissance Periodization per-muscle guides (Dec 2023–Jan 2024, Israetel/RP; evidence-informed expert guidance): rest "typically 1–3 minutes" for chest and back; "30 seconds to 3 minutes depending on the exercise" for quads; "30 seconds to 2 minutes" for triceps; "between 15 seconds and 2 minutes" for rear delts, with the rule that rest should be long enough to answer four recovery questions (target muscle, nervous system, cardiorespiratory, synergists). — [RP chest guide](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy); [RP quad guide](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/quad-training-tips-hypertrophy); [RP triceps guide](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/triceps-hypertrophy-training-tips); [RP rear-delt guide](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/rear-delt-hypertrophy-training-tips)
- Reynolds et al. (2006) used 5-min rests in RM testing and argue "Rest periods of less than 5 minutes are likely to be too short" for maximal-effort test sets because creatine-phosphate resynthesis has a fast component (<2 min, 80–90% of recovery) plus a slow component of 45–90 s more. — [Reynolds et al. 2006, JSCR (UNM PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- App defaults: Strong app: "The default Rest Timer for all exercises is 2:00" and it can be changed per exercise, with separate durations for warm-up and working sets. — [Strong Help Center](https://help.strongapp.io/article/231-rest-timer). Hevy: the timer is user-set "anything from five seconds to five minutes", adjustable in 15-s steps mid-workout, with a user-chosen default in settings (no factory default is published). — [Hevy rest timer feature page](https://www.hevyapp.com/features/workout-rest-timer/)

### Inferences
- Defensible app defaults (inference from the sources above): heavy compound strength work (≤6 reps, barbell squat/bench/deadlift/press) 180 s, allowed range 120–300 s; compound hypertrophy work (6–15 reps) 120 s, range 90–180 s; isolation and machine work (10–20 reps) 60–90 s; small-muscle isolation (rear delts, calves, forearms, abs) 45–60 s. Never default below 60 s for growth-oriented sets (Singer 2024); ≥120 s for trained lifters chasing 1RM strength (Grgic 2018; Schoenfeld 2016).
- A single global default of 120 s (as Strong does) is consistent with the evidence for most sets; per-exercise-type defaults add value mainly for heavy compounds (longer) and small isolation lifts (shorter, time-saving with no demonstrated hypertrophy cost above 60–90 s).
- Because the evidence is about minimum rest, an app should treat the timer as a floor ("rest at least X") and let sets start later; auto-regulating rest by exercise type and rep range is a reasonable heuristic, not a validated algorithm.

### Gaps
- No study directly compares 90 s vs 120 s vs 180 s for compound vs isolation exercises; the "compound vs isolation" split in the recommendations is expert extrapolation (RP) rather than a meta-analytic finding.
- A 2025 medRxiv preprint (<60 s vs >60 s in trained males) could not be retrieved (HTTP 403), so its effect sizes are not reported here.
- Hevy's and Fitbod's factory-default rest durations could not be verified (Hevy documentation gives only the adjustable range; Fitbod's help page returned 403).

## Q2. Progression: double progression, linear progression, RPE/RIR autoregulation, increments, deloads

### Takeaway
Linear progression programs add a fixed 2.5 kg (upper) / 5 kg (lower) per session or cycle and deload ~10% after repeated failure; double progression fills a rep range (e.g., 8–12) on all sets before adding the smallest available increment; RPE/RIR autoregulation targets 1–4 RIR and adjusts load ~2% per 0.5 RPE of deviation (Helms 2018). Position-stand guidance is 2–10% load increases (ACSM 2009) or 1–2 kg upper / 2–5 kg lower body for less-trained and 2–5+ kg / 5–7+ kg for more-trained lifters (NSCA "2-for-2" rule).

### Cited Findings
- ACSM Position Stand (2009): progress load by "2–10% increase in load" once the lifter can perform the current workload "for one to two repetitions over the desired number" on consecutive sessions; novices train 8–12 RM at 2–3 d/wk, intermediates 3–4 d/wk, advanced 4–5 d/wk. — [ACSM 2009 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19204579)
- NSCA "2-for-2 rule" (Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning, as summarised by PT Pioneer's CSCS chapter-17 guide): "When someone performs 2 more reps over the assigned goal in the last set of the last two workouts on an exercise, weight needs to be added for the following workout." Load increases: less-trained athletes upper body 2–5 lb (≈1–2 kg), lower body 5–10 lb (≈2–5 kg); more-trained athletes upper body 5–10+ lb (≈2–5+ kg), lower body 10–15+ lb (≈5–7+ kg); "Relative loads going up by 2.5–10% are typically used in place of absolute values." — [PT Pioneer CSCS Chapter 17](https://www.ptpioneer.com/personal-training/certifications/nsca-cscs/cscs-chapter-17/)
- StrongLifts 5×5 (program author guidance, expert opinion): squat +2.5–5 kg per increment; deadlift +5 kg initially then 2.5 kg; bench, overhead press and row +1.25–2.5 kg; women progress better on presses with 2.5-lb (≈1.25 kg) jumps; add weight every workout at first, then every 2–3–5 workouts as gains slow; "Use realistic increments that your body can handle... Get a pair of small plates" (fractional/micro plates). — [StrongLifts: How to Progress](https://stronglifts.com/stronglifts-5x5/progress/). App defaults: weight increases every workout after a completed 5×5 (e.g. 200 lb → 205 lb); "if you fail to complete all sets on an exercise for three sessions in a row, the weight will decrease by 10% next time"; the user can instead deload after 1–2 failures and by >20%, or "add 2.5lb every three workouts". — [StrongLifts app progression settings](https://support.stronglifts.com/article/71-progression)
- GZCLP (Cody Lefever's program as documented by Massiv; expert opinion): T1 main lift 5×3+ (last set AMRAP), increments "5 lb / 2.5 kg" upper and "10 lb / 5 kg" lower; on failure move to 6×2 at the same load, then 10×1, then test a new max and "restart 5×3 at about 85% of it". T2 3×10 → on failure 3×8 → 3×6 → restart 3×10 slightly above the previous starting load. T3 3×15+ with the rule "add the smallest increment when the last AMRAP set reaches 25 reps"; if 3×15 cannot be completed "reduce the load about 10–15%". T1 starts at ~85% of a tested 5RM. — [Massiv GZCLP progression](https://massiv.app/blog/gzclp-progression/)
- Wendler 5/3/1 (expert opinion): training max (TM) = ~90% of true 1RM; 4-week cycle Week 1 65/75/85% × 5/5/5+, Week 2 70/80/90% × 3/3/3+, Week 3 75/85/95% × 5/3/1+, Week 4 deload 40/50/60% × 5; the final AMRAP set is the main stimulus; add 5 lb (2.5 kg) upper / 10 lb (5 kg) lower to the TM each cycle; "Start too light, progress slowly." — [BarBend 5/3/1 guide](https://barbend.com/5-3-1-program/); [GymNote 5/3/1 percentages](https://www.gymnoteplus.com/blog/531-workout-program)
- nSuns 5/3/1 LP (community program, expert opinion): TM adjustment is driven by reps on the 95% "1+" set: 0–1 reps → no change; 2–3 reps → +2.5 kg (5 lb); 4–5 reps → +5 kg (10 lb); 6+ reps → +7.5 kg (15 lb). — [GymNote nSuns guide](https://www.gymnoteplus.com/blog/nsuns-531-lp); [Fitness Volt nSuns guide](https://fitnessvolt.com/nsuns-program/)
- Reddit PPL by Metallicadpa (community program, expert opinion): compound lifts 5/5+ AMRAP structure (five sets, last set AMRAP); squat and deadlift +5 kg, bench/OHP/row +2.5 kg when the AMRAP target is hit; fail the same weight 3 consecutive sessions → reduce 10% and work back up; accessories use double progression 8 → 12 reps then add weight and drop back to 8. — [Lift Vault Metallicadpa PPL](https://liftvault.com/programs/strength/metallicadpa-ppl-template/); [RepCheck Reddit PPL guide](https://repcheckapp.com/blog/reddit-ppl-guide)
- Double progression definition (Alpha Progression glossary, app vendor): "A progression method in which you first increase repetitions within a defined rep range then increase the load"; example 3 sets of 8–12 at 60 kg, advance when 12 reps are reached in every set, then 62.5 kg where reps "may initially fall to 10, 9, and 8"; "if the jump between available weights is large, microloading or a wider repetition range may make the transition manageable"; the method is "a practical tool, not a physiological law" and inflated reps from looser form must be guarded against. — [Alpha Progression: double progression](https://alphaprogression.com/en/glossary/double-progression)
- RIR-based RPE scale (Zourdos et al. 2016, JSCR): validated in 29 squatters; RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR, etc.; inverse relationship between bar velocity and RPE r = −0.88 (experienced) and r = −0.77 (novice); experienced lifters rated their 1RM closer to 10 than novices. — [Zourdos et al. 2016 (Europe PMC, PMID 26049792)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26049792); practical application paper: [Helms et al. 2016, Strength Cond J (PMC4961270)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)
- Helms et al. (2018), Frontiers in Physiology, RCT of RPE-based vs %1RM loading over 8 weeks (both groups gained strength; RPE group 79% probability of greater squat gains, 57% for bench; hypertrophy similar). Load-adjustment rule used: "for every 0.5 RPE above or below the upper or lower RPE threshold, respectively, load was decreased or increased by 2%"; e.g. with a target of RPE 6–8: actual 8.5 → −2%, 9 → −4%, 9.5 → −6%, 10 → −8%, actual 1–5 → +4–20%. Also: "trained lifters reported a 7 RPE when performing eight repetitions with 70% of 1RM". Scale wording: "10 indicates the maximal number of repetitions ... 9.5 indicates no further RIR but the same number of repetitions could be performed with a slightly heavier load, 9 indicates one RIR, 8.5 indicates 1–2 RIR, 8 indicates 2 RIR". — [Helms et al. 2018, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/full)
- MASS Research Review "RPE and RIR: the complete guide" (2023): recommends ~2% load change per 0.5 RPE outside the target; trained lifters are "strikingly accurate" at rating RPE on low-rep sets ≥80% 1RM but were off by 5.15 ± 2.92 reps when estimating RIR far from failure at 70% 1RM (over-estimating remaining reps; Zourdos et al. 2019); practical targets ~7–8 RPE for volume/hypertrophy work and ~9 RPE for 2–4-rep sets at 85–90% 1RM. — [MASS RPE/RIR guide](https://massresearchreview.com/2023/05/22/rpe-and-rir-the-complete-guide/)
- Proximity to failure (Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine, meta-regressions): "Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure" (negative slope for estimated RIR on hypertrophy; null for strength); authors caution the analysis is exploratory. — [Robinson et al. 2024 (Europe PMC, PMID 38970765)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38970765)
- RP mesocycle rule (expert guidance, Jan 2024): start a mesocycle at 3–4 RIR and "keep reps stable from week to week while letting your RIR decline" to 0 RIR (1 RIR on barbell lifts) in the final week; then deload. The 2017 RP framework: start each block at MEV, add 1–3 sets per week toward MRV, then deload at maintenance volume (~6 sets/muscle). — [RP quad guide](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/quad-training-tips-hypertrophy); [RP volume landmarks 2017](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)
- Deload evidence (Coleman et al. 2024, PeerJ, 39 trained participants): a 1-week complete deload at the midpoint of a 9-week high-volume program produced no difference in lower-body hypertrophy, endurance or power, but the continuous-training group gained more isometric and dynamic lower-body strength. — [Coleman et al. 2024 (Europe PMC, PMID 38274324)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38274324)
- Reps-to-%1RM relationships that drive "next weight" suggestions: Reynolds et al. (2006) found mean loads of 87.45% (5RM), 75.65% (10RM) and 61.61% (20RM) of 1RM for barbell bench press, and 85.91%, 70.10%, 51.58% for plate-loaded leg press; the decrease is nonlinear. — [Reynolds et al. 2006 (UNM PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf). Shimano et al. (2006): more reps are completed at 60% 1RM in the back squat than in bench press or arm curl (significant intensity × exercise interaction), with smaller differences at 80–90%; trained vs untrained differed only for bench press at 90%. — [Shimano et al. 2006 (Europe PMC, PMID 17194239)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17194239)

### Inferences
- Implementable suggestion logic (double progression), consistent with the sources: for a template "3×8–12": if all sets reached 12 (or the top of the range) with acceptable form → next session add the exercise's smallest increment (barbell 2.5 kg; dumbbells typically 2 kg per hand, sometimes 2.5 kg; pin-loaded machines one plate, typically 5 kg, or a 2.5 kg add-on plate if available); if at least one set fell below the bottom of the range for 2–3 consecutive sessions → hold or drop ~5–10%; otherwise keep the load and add reps. The message "last time you hit 3×10 at 30 kg, try 32.5 kg" is the top-of-range case; note that 30 → 32.5 kg is an 8.3% jump (above the 2.5–5% NSCA guidance for upper-body lifts), so for light loads the app should prefer 1.25 kg / 1 kg micro-increments or widen the rep range, and should expect reps to fall to roughly 7–8 after such a jump (Epley-inverse: with e1RM 40 kg, 32.5 kg predicts ~7 reps).
- Percent-based increments generalise better across equipment than fixed kg: upper-body core lifts +2.5–5%, lower-body core lifts +5–10% (NSCA/ACSM), rounded to the equipment's minimum step. For a 73 kg intermediate, that is ~2.5 kg on a 60–100 kg bench and 5 kg on a 100–150 kg squat, matching the program conventions above.
- Linear progression for novices (StrongLifts/GZCLP/PPL) can be implemented as: +2.5 kg upper / +5 kg lower per successful session; on failure hold the load; after 3 consecutive failures deload 10% (StrongLifts/PPL) or shift rep scheme (GZCLP cascade). Switch to weekly (5/3/1-style: +2.5/+5 kg per 4-week cycle on a 90% TM) once session-to-session jumps fail repeatedly.
- RPE autoregulation for intermediates: prescribe reps and a target RPE (7–8 for hypertrophy volume work, 8–9 for strength work), adjust the next set/session by ±2% per 0.5 RPE of deviation, and cap adjustments (e.g. ±8%); since lifters over-estimate RIR far from failure (MASS 2023), the app should trust RPE ratings ≥8 more than ratings ≤6.
- Deload logic: RP-style planned deloads at the end of a mesocycle (after volume reaches MRV or performance stalls) are expert practice; the only RCT (Coleman 2024) found a full week off cost some strength and did not add hypertrophy, so an app should default to reduced-volume deloads (e.g. 5/3/1's 40–60% week, or RP's ~half volume) rather than complete rest, and trigger them on stalls/fatigue rather than on a fixed calendar.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed trial compares double progression vs linear progression vs RPE-based progression head-to-head in recreational lifters; increment sizes for dumbbells and machines are equipment conventions and coach opinion, not experimental findings.
- The exact 5/3/1 and nSuns rules were verified from secondary program guides (BarBend, GymNote, Fitness Volt, Lift Vault), not from the authors' books/spreadsheets.

## Q3. Estimated 1RM: formulas, accuracy by rep range and exercise, bodyweight-plus-load movements, unilateral/machine work

### Takeaway
Epley, Brzycki, Lander, O'Conner, Lombardi, Mayhew and Wathen all correlate r > 0.95 with measured 1RM when ≤10 reps are used; accuracy is best with 3–5 reps (Reynolds 2006: 5RM R² = 0.993 bench, 0.974 leg press) and degrades beyond 10 reps, where linear equations (Brzycki, Lander) can badly over-estimate. Equations are exercise-specific: deadlift 1RM is systematically under-predicted, and light isolation/dumbbell/cable lifts behave differently from heavy barbell lifts (a 2026 large-scale preprint fits a load-dependent conversion factor). For weighted pull-ups/dips, compute e1RM on total system load (bodyweight + added load; ExRx suggests ~91–93% of bodyweight for precision) and report the added-load equivalent by subtracting bodyweight.

### Cited Findings
- Formulas (w = load, r = reps): Epley w·(1 + r/30) (Epley 1985 poundage chart); Brzycki w·36/(37 − r) ≡ w/(1.0278 − 0.0278r) (Brzycki 1993); Lombardi w·r^0.10 (1989); O'Conner w·(1 + 0.025r) (1989); Mayhew w/(0.522 + 0.419·e^(−0.055r)) (1992); Wathen w/(0.4880 + 0.538·e^(−0.075r)) (1994); Lander w/(1.013 − 0.0267123r) (1985). Wikipedia notes Epley and Brzycki "return identical results for 10 repetitions" and diverge at lower reps, that estimates "may vary by 10% or more", and that exercise-specific formulas improve accuracy. — [Wikipedia: One-repetition maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-repetition_maximum); equation table with references (Adams, Berger, Brown, Brzycki, Cummings & Finn, Kemmler, Lander, Lombardi, Mayhew, O'Connor, Reynolds, Tucker, Wathen, Welday) in [Mayhew et al. 2008, JSCR 22(5):1570–1577 (PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478PredictionAccuracy.pdf)
- Reynolds, Gordon & Robergs (2006), JSCR, 70 subjects (34 M/36 F, 18–69 y) tested 1, 5, 10, 20RM on barbell bench press (CP) and plate-loaded leg press (LP): the 5RM predicted 1RM best, R² = 0.993 (CP) and 0.974 (LP); R² for 10RM/20RM were 0.976/0.955 (CP) and 0.933/0.915 (LP); derived equations 1RM_CP = 1.1307·5RM + 0.6999 (SEE 2.98 kg) and 1RM_LP = 1.0970·5RM + 14.2546 (SEE 16.16 kg); "no more than 10 repetitions should be used in linear equations"; in cross-validation at 5RM, Reynolds/Abadie/Epley had the smallest residuals for LP while Brzycki, Lander and O'Connor under-estimated LP; Reynolds, Brzycki and O'Connor were most accurate for CP; nonlinear Lombardi and Mayhew were "less accurate than all linear equations" at 5RM; adding height, weight, FFM, girths, sex or training volume did not improve prediction; "prediction equations must be exercise specific" and their LP equation should not be applied to other leg-press machines. A 5-kg error in the 5RM load produces 19.7 kg (LP) / 6.3 kg (CP) error in predicted 1RM. — [Reynolds et al. 2006 (UNM PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf); abstract at [Europe PMC PMID 16937972](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16937972)
- Mayhew et al. (2008), JSCR, 103 college women, bench press, reps at 60–90% 1RM before/after 12 weeks training: with the full 2–30-rep range, linear Brzycki and Lander equations over-estimated wildly (mean % error 26.7 ± 101.7% and 22.9 ± 70.7%, ICC 0.24–0.47), while Mayhew (1.2 ± 9.0%), O'Connor (−2.1 ± 9.0%), Kemmler, Lombardi, Reynolds, Tucker and Wathen had ICC ≥ 0.93; restricting to ≤10 reps brought nearly every equation within ±5% mean error (e.g. Brzycki −2.0 ± 10.5%, Epley-type Welday 0.5 ± 10.2%, Wathen 0.7 ± 10.6%). "Prediction equations were more accurate ... in which fewer than 10 RTF were used." Individual SDs of ~9–10% remain even with ≤10 reps. — [Mayhew et al. 2008 (PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478PredictionAccuracy.pdf)
- LeSuer et al. (1997), JSCR (secondary summaries; original not retrievable): 67 untrained college students, bench press, squat and deadlift with <10 reps; all seven equations (Brzycki, Epley, Lander, Lombardi, Mayhew, O'Conner, Wathen) correlated r > 0.95 with actual 1RM; "all seven equations significantly underestimated the deadlift 1RM"; Epley had the lowest squat error (~3%); only Mayhew and Wathen predictions did not differ significantly from measured 1RM in the aggregate, the others under-estimating by up to ~6%. — [OneRepMaxCalculators accuracy guide](https://onerepmaxcalculators.com/guides/one-rep-max-basics/how-accurate-are-1rm-calculators/); [UKDiss summary of LeSuer 1997](https://ukdiss.com/examples/one-repetition-maximum-rft.php). The same secondary guide synthesises typical error margins of ±3–5% for 1–5 reps, ±5–8% for 6–10 reps and ±10–15% above 10 reps, and per-lift errors of ~3–5% (bench), ~5–7% (squat), 8–10% under-estimation (deadlift). (Secondary synthesis; treat as approximate.)
- Exercise specificity of reps at a given %1RM: Shimano et al. (2006) — more reps at 60% 1RM in back squat than bench press or arm curl (significant intensity × exercise interaction). — [Shimano et al. 2006 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17194239)
- Marzagao (2026), arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed), Fitbod data: 303,494 near-failure sets from 14,966 users across 388 exercises; classical equations use a fixed conversion factor (Epley k = 30, Brzycki k ≈ 36, Wathen/Mayhew k ≈ 29) whereas the fitted factor rises with load: k(w) = −2.55 + 4.58·ln(w), giving k ≈ 8 at 10 kg (lateral raise), 9.9 at 15 kg (curl), 12.2 at 25 kg (DB bench), 15.8 at 55 kg (lat pulldown), 16.9 at 70 kg (barbell bench), 17.5 at 80 kg (squat), 20.4 at 150 kg (deadlift). Proposed formula 1RM = w·(1 + (r − 1)^0.85 / (−2.55 + 4.58·ln w)) reduced within-person inconsistency by 17.6% vs Brzycki, 17.4% vs Epley, 17.0% vs Wathen, 21.9% vs Mayhew; improvement was larger for isolation (21.9%) than compound (16.3%) exercises and by equipment: dumbbell 22.1%, cable 18.9%, machine 16.0%, barbell 12.3%; correlation between mean exercise load and improvement r = −0.61. Example: 13 kg dumbbell curl × 10 → Brzycki ≈ 17 kg vs proposed ≈ 22 kg; 100 kg bench × 5 → agree within 2 kg. Logging convention: dumbbell load logged per hand, barbell total including bar, machine/cable as stack setting. — [arXiv 2603.17495 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17495); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495)
- Bodyweight-plus-load exercises: ExRx "Calculating actual resistance" gives three precision levels for pull-ups/chin-ups/dips — simple "Total Body = 100%", moderate "Body − 2 Arms = 100% − 2(4.715%) = 90.57%", advanced (with upper-arm torque) ≈ 92.8% of bodyweight; with added weight, compute total actual resistance, then "subtract the weight of the body" to get the external load to use; it suggests a 5% increase in actual resistance once 12 reps are achieved. — [ExRx: Calculating Actual Resistance](https://exrx.net/WeightTraining/Bodyweight)
- Calculator conventions: King of Weighted's calculator instructs "For weighted calisthenics exercises dips, pull/chin ups and muscle ups add your body weight in the bodyweight field" and offers Epley, Brzycki and Lander. — [King of Weighted 1RM calculator](https://www.kingofweighted.com/calculator). StrengthCalculator's pull-up tool computes 1RM = (bodyweight + added weight)·(1 + reps/30) and then subtracts bodyweight to report the belt load (e.g. 80 kg + 20 kg × 5 reps → ≈117 kg total, i.e. +37 kg added). — [StrengthCalculator pull-up calculator](https://strengthcalculator.org/pull-up-calculator)
- Load actually lifted in push-ups (for tracking bodyweight pressing): Suprak, Dawes & Stephenson (2011), JSCR, 28 trained men on a force plate: traditional push-up 69.16% of body mass in the up position and 75.04% in the down position; knee (modified) push-up 53.56% and 61.80%. — [Suprak et al. 2011 (PubMed 20179649)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20179649/). Ebben et al. (2011): feet-elevated push-ups produce higher ground-reaction force than all other variants; hands-elevated and knee push-ups lower; "subject height was not related to the GRF" except hands elevated on a 61-cm box (r = 0.63); loads can be "quantif[ied] ... as a percentage of body mass". — [Ebben et al. 2011 (Europe PMC, PMID 21873902)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21873902)
- Alternative prediction methods: Sigvaldsen et al. (2023), PLOS One, Smith-machine bench press and prone bench pull: reps-to-failure equations had SEE 8.6 kg (bench) and 6.4 kg (pull) vs load-velocity SEE 3.2–4.5 kg; all methods ICC > 0.96 but "cannot be used interchangeably". — [Sigvaldsen et al. 2023, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0288649)

### Inferences
- Default e1RM formula: Epley (or Brzycki for a more conservative training max) applied only to sets of ≤10 reps, ideally 3–6 reps, with the app flagging estimates from >10-rep sets as low-confidence (±10–15%) and from >15 reps as unreliable; store the rep count with every e1RM so trend charts can filter to comparable rep ranges.
- Exercise-type handling: (a) barbell compounds — Epley/Brzycki are fine; (b) deadlift — expect ~8–10% under-prediction (LeSuer 1997 summaries), so an e1RM from deadlift reps is a floor; (c) light dumbbell/cable/machine isolation — classical formulas under-count the value of each rep (Marzagao 2026), so either use the load-dependent formula above or accept that e1RM on isolation lifts is only useful for within-exercise trends, not cross-exercise comparison; (d) unilateral machine/dumbbell work (e.g. one-arm triceps extension) — no validation literature exists; treat the per-limb load as w, compute e1RM per side with the same formula, keep it as a trend metric only, and never sum sides.
- Weighted dips/pull-ups: compute e1RM_total = f(bodyweight_at_session + added load, reps) and display both the total and the "added" equivalent (e1RM_total − bodyweight). Using 100% bodyweight (simple, matches every consumer calculator) is adequate for trends; the 90.6–92.8% ExRx factors matter only if comparing to a barbell lift. Because the relative load changes when bodyweight changes, the app must log bodyweight per session (or interpolate) for bodyweight exercises; for unweighted pull-ups/push-ups, track total system load × reps or e1RM_total, and for push-ups use ~69–75% of bodyweight (Suprak 2011) as the load.
- A percent-of-1RM table for load suggestions should be exercise-specific: use Reynolds' bench (87/76/62% at 5/10/20RM) and leg-press (86/70/52%) values rather than one generic table, and expect squats to allow more reps at a given % than bench or curls (Shimano 2006).

### Gaps
- The original LeSuer 1997 paper could not be fetched (Semantic Scholar rate-limited, PubMed CAPTCHA); the per-lift error percentages above come from secondary summaries and should be verified before hard-coding.
- No peer-reviewed validation of any 1RM equation for weighted pull-ups/dips or for unilateral machine exercises was found; the bodyweight-inclusion rule is a biomechanical convention (ExRx) and calculator practice, not an experimental finding.
- The 2026 load-dependent formula is a preprint validated by internal consistency on app data, not against measured 1RMs.

## Q4. Weekly volume landmarks per muscle group and how to count sets

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses show a graded dose-response: <5, 5–9 and 10+ weekly sets per muscle yield ~5.4%, 6.6% and 9.8% growth (Schoenfeld 2017), 12–20 sets/week is a reasonable optimum for trained lifters (Baz-Valle 2022), a minimum of ~4 sets/week still works (Iversen 2021), and the 2025 Pelland meta-regression finds continued but diminishing returns for hypertrophy (much steeper diminishing returns for strength) with indirect sets best counted as 0.5. RP's 2023–24 per-muscle landmarks (intermediates) mostly put MEV at 4–8 and MRV at 16–30 sets/week, with much lower numbers for hamstrings, front delts, traps, abs and rear delts.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), Journal of Sports Sciences, meta-analysis of 15 studies / 34 groups: "Each additional set was associated with an increase in effect size (ES) of 0.023 corresponding to an increase in the percentage gain by 0.37%"; higher vs lower volume ES difference 0.241 (3.9% gain difference); categories <5, 5–9, 10+ sets/week trend p = 0.074. — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (Europe PMC, PMID 27433992)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27433992). The categorical percentage gains were 5.4%, 6.6% and 9.8% for <5, 5–9 and 10+ weekly sets. — [Built With Science summary of Schoenfeld 2017](https://builtwithscience.com/fitness-tips/how-many-sets-per-muscle-group-per-week/); full-text PDF: [ageingmuscle.be mirror](https://www.ageingmuscle.be/sites/bams/files/publications/Dose%20response%20relationship%20between%20weekly%20resistance%20training%20volume%20and%20increases.pdf)
- Ralston et al. (2017), Sports Medicine, strength meta-analysis of 9 studies / 61 groups: low weekly sets ES 0.82 (95% CI 0.47–1.17) vs high weekly sets ES 1.01 (0.70–1.32), difference 0.18 (0.06–0.30; p = 0.003); multi-joint LWS 0.81 vs HWS 1.00 (p = 0.04); isolation LWS 0.95 vs HWS 1.10 (p = 0.008); "graded dose-response relationship between weekly sets performed and strength gain", medium and high volumes more effective than low. — [Ralston et al. 2017 (Europe PMC, PMID 28755103)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28755103)
- Baz-Valle et al. (2022), Journal of Human Kinetics, trained men 18–35 y: no difference between moderate (12–20 sets/week) and high (>20) volume for quadriceps (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59); high volume better for triceps (p = 0.01); "a range of 12–20 weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation". — [Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (Europe PMC, PMID 35291645)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35291645)
- Schoenfeld et al. (2019), MSSE, RCT in 34 trained men: 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise, 3×/week for 8 weeks — strength and endurance gains did not differ between groups ("just three 13-min weekly sessions"), but elbow-flexor, mid-thigh and lateral-thigh thickness increased more with higher volume. — [Schoenfeld et al. 2019 (Europe PMC, PMID 30153194)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30153194)
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos (2025/2026), Sports Medicine, meta-regressions of 67 studies / 2,058 participants: posterior probability that volume increases both hypertrophy and strength = 100%, with diminishing returns "considerably more pronounced" for strength; frequency has negligible effect on hypertrophy but strength gains increase with frequency (diminishing returns); sets were classified as direct vs indirect and three counting methods tested — total (indirect = 1), fractional (indirect = 0.5), direct only (indirect = 0) — and "the fractional method showed strongest evidence"; "Distinguishing between direct and indirect sets appears essential for predicting adaptations". — [Pelland et al. (Europe PMC, PMID 41343037)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41343037); preprint: [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Iversen et al. (2021): "minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group using a 6–15 RM loading range (15–40 repetitions can be used if training is performed to volitional failure)". — [Iversen et al. 2021 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34125411)
- RP definitions (Israetel, 4 Jan 2017 article): MV ≈ 6 sets/week per muscle maintains size when trained ≥2×/week; MEV = lowest volume that produces growth and the starting point of a block; MAV = productive range between MEV and MRV; MRV = the ceiling beyond which recovery fails. Counting rule: count only "sets where the target muscle is the prime mover or isolation exercises specifically targeting that muscle"; working sets assume 30–85% 1RM, 5–30 reps, 0–4 RIR; progress by adding 1–3 sets/week from MEV toward MRV, then deload at ~MV. — [RP: Training Volume Landmarks for Muscle Growth (2017)](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)
- RP per-muscle landmarks, republished Dec 2023–Jan 2024 (sets per week; "averages for serious, intermediate lifters" with 3–7 years of whole-body training; MAV*P/MRV*P apply when the muscle is a priority). Expert guidance, not a study:
  - Chest (3 Jan 2024): MV 2–4, MEV 4–6, MAV 6–16, MRV 16–24, MAV*P 16–24, MRV*P 24–32+; frequency 2–4×/week; compound presses 8–12 reps, flyes 10–20; all horizontal pressing, incline pressing and isolation count. — [RP chest](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy)
  - Quads (3 Jan 2024): MV 2–4, MEV 4–6, MAV 6–14, MRV 14–18, MAV*P 10–18, MRV*P 18–24+; 2–5 sessions/week; ~50% of volume at 10–20 reps, rest split between 5–10 and 20–30. — [RP quads](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/quad-training-tips-hypertrophy)
  - Hamstrings (29 Dec 2023): MV 0–2, MEV 2–4, MAV 2–8, MRV 8–14, MAV*P 8–14, MRV*P 14–20; 2–3 sessions/week; "substantially lower than for most other muscles". — [RP hamstrings](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/hamstring-hypertrophy-training-tips)
  - Glutes (3 Jan 2024): MV 2–6, MEV 6–8, MAV 8–24, MRV 24–30, MAV*P 24–30, MRV*P 30–40+; 2–5 sessions/week; "glutes can get enough work to grow robustly ... through proper quad and hamstring training", especially high-hip-flexion lifts. — [RP glutes](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/glute-hypertrophy-training-tips)
  - Calves (4 Jan 2024): MV 2–4, MEV 4–6, MAV 6–16, MRV 16–24, MAV*P 16–24, MRV*P 24–32+; 3–6 sessions/week. — [RP calves](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/calves-hypertrophy-training-tips)
  - Side delts (3 Jan 2024): MV 2–6, MEV 6–8, MAV 8–24, MRV 24–30, MAV*P 24–30, MRV*P 30–40+; 3–6 sessions/week. — [RP side delts](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/side-delt-hypertrophy-training-tips)
  - Rear delts (3 Jan 2024): MV 0–4, MEV 0–4, MAV 4–12, MRV 12–20, MAV*P 24–30, MRV*P 30–40+; 3–6 sessions/week; back training often provides sufficient stimulus. — [RP rear delts](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/rear-delt-hypertrophy-training-tips)

  - Biceps (31 Dec 2023): MV 6–8, MEV 8–10, MAV 14–20, MRV 20–26, MAV*P 20–26, MRV*P 26–35; 3–6 sessions/week; "The more back training you perform, the less direct bicep work you may need". — [RP biceps](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/bicep-hypertrophy-training-tips)
  - Triceps (3 Jan 2024): MV 0–4, MEV 4–6, MAV 6–16, MRV 16–20, MAV*P 16–20, MRV*P 20–26+; 2–4 sessions/week in the context of normal chest training. — [RP triceps](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/triceps-hypertrophy-training-tips)

- How apps count sets: Hevy's guidance article recommends to "count the primary muscle as one set and the secondary muscles as half a set" (lat pulldown = 1 set lats/upper back + 0.5 biceps), calling it "a good rule of thumb"; it suggests 10–20 weekly sets for back/chest/quads and 6–10 for shoulders, biceps, triceps, hamstrings, calves; Hevy shows set counts per muscle group under Profile > Statistics. — [Hevy: How many sets per muscle group](https://www.hevyapp.com/how-many-sets/)

### Inferences
- For a general strength + physique intermediate, a defensible default target band per muscle is 10–20 fractional sets/week (Schoenfeld 2017 10+; Baz-Valle 12–20; RP MAV bands), with a floor alert at <4 direct sets/week (Iversen) and a ceiling warning around RP's MRV (16–24 for chest/quads/triceps/calves, 24–30 for glutes/side delts/biceps, 8–14 for hamstrings, 8–12 for front delts, 12–20 for rear delts/traps/abs).
- Set counting that matches the best-supported method (Pelland 2025): primary mover = 1.0, secondary movers = 0.5, and count only working sets (≥ ~30% 1RM, ≤4 RIR per RP). Map compound lifts to muscles once (e.g. bench → chest 1.0, triceps 0.5, front delts 0.5; row → back 1.0, biceps 0.5, rear delts 0.5; squat → quads 1.0, glutes 0.5; RDL → hamstrings 1.0, glutes 0.5). RP's own guides support treating presses as sufficient front-delt volume and rows as partial biceps/rear-delt volume, which is what the 0.5 rule encodes.
- Because RP revised its landmarks downward in 2023–24 (e.g. chest MEV 4–6, quads MRV 14–18), an app should not hard-code the widely copied older tables (chest MEV 8–10, quads MRV 20+); the per-muscle bands above are the current published version.
- Strength-focused users get little extra strength from volume beyond moderate levels (Schoenfeld 2019; Pelland: steep diminishing returns for strength), so a "strength" template can sit near 6–12 hard sets/week per lift-pattern while a "physique" template targets 12–20.

### Gaps
- RP's back-muscle numeric landmarks are only published as an image and could not be extracted; the RP Help Center pages (which mirror the app) returned HTTP 403.
- The Pelland 2025 abstract does not state numeric inflection points (e.g. sets per session or per week where returns flatten); the full paper (Springer, paywalled/redirect) was not accessible.
- No published data on how Strong, Fitbod or the RP Hypertrophy app internally weight secondary muscles; only Hevy's editorial guidance (0.5) was found.

## Q5. Bodyweight and height: allometric scaling, relative strength, limb-length effects, and whether height-adjusted standards exist

### Takeaway
Theory and lab data support normalising strength to body mass with an exponent near 2/3 (Jaric 2002: 0.67 for force; Folland 2008: 0.66 in lean subjects, lower (~0.45) in fatter subjects), and ratio scaling (load ÷ bodyweight) is biased against heavier lifters; empirical exponents in elite powerlifters are even lower (0.35–0.52 by lift; Montenegro 2026), and standard handicaps (Wilks, IPF GL) use bodyweight only. Arm length, torso girth and reach/height ratios measurably affect bench and deadlift, but no validated height- or limb-length-adjusted strength standard exists.

### Cited Findings
- Jaric (2002), Sports Medicine review: recommends an allometric index strength / body_mass^b with "b = 0.67 for muscle force" (dynamometer/lift) or "b = 1 for muscle torque"; ratio scaling and no scaling are criticised as incorrect ways to account for body size. — [Jaric 2002 (Europe PMC, PMID 12141882)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12141882)
- Folland et al. (2008), European Journal of Applied Physiology, 86 young men, knee-extensor strength: fat-free mass explained 31% (force) and 52–58% (torque) of strength variance; for lean, homogeneous groups (<20% body fat) exponents of 0.66 (force) and 1.0 (torque) fit; for adipose groups (>20% fat) lower exponents fit (force 0.45, torque 0.68); scaling to fat-free mass gives 0.76 (force) and 1.12 (torque). — [Folland et al. 2008 (Europe PMC, PMID 18172672)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18172672)
- Vanderburgh & Batterham (1999), MSSE, validation of the Wilks formula on IPF world-championship lifters (30 men, 27 women per lift): no body-mass bias for bench press or total; favourable bias toward intermediate classes in women's squat; "linear unfavorable bias toward heavier men and women in the DL"; Wilks judged valid for bench and total. — [Vanderburgh & Batterham 1999 (Europe PMC, PMID 10613442)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10613442)
- Cleather (2006), JSCR, IPF 1995–2004 data: performance vs body mass best modelled as y = a − b·x^(−c) (lift-specific constants); this model showed no body-mass bias, whereas allometric fits were biased toward intermediate weight categories. — [Cleather 2006 (Europe PMC, PMID 16686573)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16686573)
- Montenegro, Wicker & Donath (2026), Frontiers in Physiology, 457,471 drug-tested powerlifters: fitted allometric exponents for the all-time top-20 cohort — squat 0.515 (M) / 0.542 (F), bench 0.345 / 0.424, deadlift 0.394 / 0.691, total 0.47 / 0.41 (bootstrap 95% CI for elite males 0.483–0.584); all significantly below the theoretical 2/3 (males t = −6.59, p < 0.001); bench shows the strongest attenuation; the empirical model was statistically equivalent to IPF GL for elite males (Spearman 0.995 within class); height/body dimensions were not measured. — [Montenegro et al. 2026, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1847605/full)
- Relative-strength norms (ratio scaling): van den Hoek et al. (2024), JSAMS, 809,986 drug-tested powerlifting entries (571,650 male): 90th-percentile relative strength (kg lifted ÷ body mass) in 18–35-year-olds — squat 2.83 (M) / 2.26 (F), bench 1.95 / 1.35, deadlift 3.25 / 2.66; at >80 years squat 1.72 / 1.01, bench 1.31 / 0.92, deadlift 2.30 / 1.68. — [van den Hoek et al. 2024 (Europe PMC, PMID 39060209)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/39060209)
- Anthropometry and lifts: Ferland et al. (2020), Int J Exerc Sci, 59 male classic powerlifters: higher relative squat/bench strength correlated with higher body weight, BMI and torso circumference (r = 0.26–0.49) and smaller lower-leg-length ratios (r = −0.31 to −0.45); torso circumference predicted absolute squat (β = 0.41), bench (0.77), deadlift (0.88) and total (0.89); reach/height positively predicted relative deadlift (β = 0.37) but negatively predicted relative bench (β = −0.25). — [Ferland et al. 2020 (Europe PMC, PMID 33414873)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33414873)
- Caruso et al. (2012), JSCR, 36 men: body mass had strong univariate correlations with bench 1RM; "total arm length was moderately associated with 1RM" (positive), while biacromial width related inversely to repetitions at lighter loads. — [Caruso et al. 2012 (Europe PMC, PMID 22027858)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22027858)
- Keogh et al. (2007), J Sports Sci, 54 powerlifters: heavier classes had greater muscle and fat mass, girths and bony breadths; middle/heavyweights had longer absolute segment lengths but "all three groups had similar Zp-scores for the segment lengths, indicating similar segment length proportions". — [Keogh et al. 2007 (Europe PMC, PMID 17786689)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17786689)
- Reynolds et al. (2006), mixed-sex sample (n = 70): height correlated r = 0.47 with leg-press 1RM and r = 0.59 with bench 1RM, but adding height/weight/FFM/girths to a 5RM-based equation did not improve 1RM prediction (5RM already captured them). — [Reynolds et al. 2006 (UNM PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- Ebben et al. (2011): subject height was unrelated to push-up ground-reaction force in all but one variant. — [Ebben et al. 2011 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21873902)
- Consumer standards use bodyweight (plus sex and age), not height: Strength Level's standards are built from user-submitted lifts by bodyweight and sex/age. — [Strength Level standards](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards). A general search found no validated height- or limb-length-adjusted formula; guides note that "traditional strength standards primarily use bodyweight-based comparisons rather than height classes". — [TrainCalc strength-standards guide (search summary)](https://traincalc.com/guides/strength-standards-guide)

### Inferences
- Definition to implement: relative strength = load ÷ bodyweight (this is how van den Hoek's norms are expressed). Caveat to show users: ratio scaling favours lighter lifters and penalises heavier ones (Jaric 2002; Vanderburgh 1999 for deadlift), so for cross-bodyweight comparison the app should also compute an allometric index load ÷ bodyweight^0.67 (or, for powerlifting-style comparison, an IPF GL/DOTS-type score). For the 73 kg reference user, 73^0.67 = 17.7; a 100 kg bench gives ratio 1.37 and allometric index 5.64.
- Exponent choice: 0.67 is the theory-backed default for a general population (Jaric; Folland lean subjects); if the user's body fat is high, a lower exponent (~0.45) fits better (Folland); elite-lifter data (0.35–0.52) suggest strength scales even less than 2/3 with mass in highly trained people, so an app comparing against competition data should use the federation formula (IPF GL) rather than a raw 2/3 exponent.
- Height: the literature supports limb-length effects (long arms/reach favour deadlift and hurt relative bench; longer lower legs hurt relative squat/bench; arm length itself positively relates to absolute bench in untrained men), but the effect is direction-specific per lift and not quantified in a way that yields a correction factor. An app can use height only qualitatively (e.g. explain why a tall lifter's bench lags) or use lift-specific ratios (Q6) instead of adjusting standards by height.
- Bodyweight is also needed for e1RM on bodyweight exercises (Q3), so the app should store a bodyweight time series and use the nearest reading for each session.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, validated height- or limb-length-adjusted strength standard or correction coefficient was found (search across scholarly and consumer sources); this appears to be a genuine absence, not a retrieval failure.
- Allometric exponents specifically for machine or isolation exercises, and for women in recreational populations, were not found.

## Q6. Body-part comparison: published strength ratios for balance assessment

### Takeaway
The only large-data, competition-verified ratios are for squat:bench:deadlift (median raw male ≈ 1.5 : 1.00 : 1.73, squat ≈ 90% of deadlift, stable across weight classes; drug-tested 90th-percentile norms imply squat ≈ 1.45× and deadlift ≈ 1.67× bench). Push/pull and accessory ratios (OHP ≈ 60–67% of bench, row ≈ bench, Poliquin's structural-balance table, the "4:3:2:1" squat-anchored framework) are coach opinion or community survey data with no peer-reviewed validation.

### Cited Findings
- OpenPowerlifting-derived medians (Fitness Volt analysis of verified raw male competition results): "squat 1.5 : bench 1.00 : deadlift 1.73", stable across classes; 74 kg class medians squat 375 lb (170 kg), bench 248 lb (112.5 kg), deadlift 446 lb (202 kg) (1.51 : 1.00 : 1.80); 83 kg class 408 / 276 / 480 lb (185 / 125 / 218 kg); the average lifter squats about 90% of their deadlift. — [Fitness Volt squat/bench/deadlift ratio analysis](https://fitnessvolt.com/strength-standards/research/squat-bench-deadlift-ratio)
- van den Hoek et al. (2024), 809,986 entries, 90th-percentile relative strength for 18–35-year-old men: squat 2.83, bench 1.95, deadlift 3.25 × bodyweight → implied ratios squat:bench 1.45, deadlift:bench 1.67, squat:deadlift 0.87. — [van den Hoek et al. 2024 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/39060209)
- Poliquin structural balance (expert opinion, 1997 T-Nation article; data "from working with hundreds and hundreds of elite athletes", author admits "this area needs further study"): close-grip bench press 100%, incline barbell press 83%, supinated chin-up 81% (bodyweight + load), behind-the-neck press 64%, Scott (preacher) curl 46%, standing reverse curl 30%, seated dumbbell external rotation 9% (8 reps); all at a 40X0 tempo. — [T-Nation: Achieving Structural Balance (Poliquin 1997)](https://archive.t-nation.com/training/achieving-structural-balance/). A later compilation of Poliquin's numbers lists different values: incline 91%, parallel-bar dip 117% (bodyweight + load), 6RM one-arm dumbbell press 30% per arm, lying triceps extension 40%, supinated chin-up 87%, Scott curl 46%, reverse curl 40%, external rotation 9.8%; lower body anchored on high-bar back squat 100%: front squat 85%, deadlift 125%, power clean 66%, power snatch 51%, close-grip bench 66% of squat; 4010 tempo. — [Peter Roberts Coaching: Structural Balance guide](http://www.peterrobertscoaching.com/blog/definitive-guide-to-structural-balance-training)
- "4:3:2:1" framework (coach reference, no citations): relative to back squat 100% — deadlift 125% (typical range 110–125%), bench 75% (typical 70–80%), barbell row 66%, overhead press 50%; extended table e.g. front squat 85%, trap-bar deadlift 130%, incline bench 67%, push press 62%, weighted pull-up (added load) 40%. — [Trainnode strength standards & lift ratios](https://trainnode.com/strength-standards)
- Overhead press vs bench: "60–67% of bench press 1RM", citing Starting Strength's 0.67 press/bench ratio and a Reddit r/weightroom survey reporting 62.5%; <55% flagged as lagging overhead strength, >75% as a bench weakness. — [StrengthCalculator OHP calculator](https://strengthcalculator.org/overhead-press-calculator)
- Row vs bench: target "1:1 row-to-bench ratio" for pressing athletes, with <0.70 flagged ("the back is not keeping up"); the page attributes this to "physio-oriented research and coaching practice" without naming studies. — [StrengthCalculator barbell row calculator](https://strengthcalculator.org/barbell-row-calculator)
- Cross-lift relationships within one exercise family: Reynolds et al. (2006) show upper- and lower-body lifts have different rep-load curves (bench 87/76/62% vs leg press 86/70/52% of 1RM at 5/10/20RM), i.e. ratios between lifts depend on the rep range used to estimate them. — [Reynolds et al. 2006 (UNM PDF)](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an app "balance" screen, the defensible tiers are: (1) evidence-grade: squat 0.85–0.90 × deadlift and bench ≈ 0.65–0.70 × squat (competition medians; flag a user whose squat:deadlift is <0.75 or >1.0, or bench:squat <0.55 or >0.85 as unusual); (2) opinion-grade, clearly labelled: OHP 0.60–0.67 × bench, row ≈ 0.9–1.0 × bench, front squat ≈ 0.85 × back squat, weighted chin-up (bodyweight + load) ≈ 0.8–0.9 × close-grip bench, weighted dip ≈ 1.1–1.2 × close-grip bench, incline ≈ 0.83–0.91 × close-grip bench.
- Ratios should be computed on e1RMs from comparable rep ranges (≤6 reps) because rep-load curves differ across lifts; for bodyweight lifts use total system load (Q3).
- Poliquin's figures were derived on elite athletes at strict tempos; for a recreational 73 kg lifter they are heuristics for spotting gross imbalances (e.g. OHP < 50% of bench, row < 70% of bench), not targets.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study validates any push/pull or accessory-lift ratio against injury risk or performance in recreational lifters; row:bench and OHP:bench figures rest on coaching practice and one Reddit survey.
- Two versions of Poliquin's structural-balance percentages circulate (1997 article vs later compilations) and disagree (chin-up 81% vs 87%, incline 83% vs 91%); the original data were never published.
- Isokinetic hamstring:quadriceps ratios (~0.6) exist in the sports-medicine literature but were outside this search; no lift-based hamstring:quad standard (e.g. RDL:squat) with evidence was found.
